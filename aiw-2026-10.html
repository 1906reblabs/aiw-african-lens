The Plans Outnumber the Contracts

A World Bank shared-compute initiative, an Egyptian data-centre plan, a Nigerian pooling proposal and South Africa's first dated policy timetable all landed in October. None came with a budget, a site and a date. This edition tracks the gap, corrects four earlier points, and uses only named public reporting.

§1Editor's Thesis

In October, four different actors described African AI compute in the language of shared infrastructure, and none of them attached a budget, a site and a date to it. The World Bank announced on 20 October a shared, pay-as-you-go GPU initiative for Africa and Latin America. Egypt's minister said the country will build five to seven "mega data centres" aimed at exports by 2030. Nigeria proposed pooling African compute. South Africa's policy team told a Pretoria audience that its drafting priorities are compute, data and model sovereignty. The working argument of this edition is that compute is being governed as a public good before it has been built as a market, and that the plans now outnumber the contracts.

The evidence is specific about what was said and equally specific about what was not. The World Bank initiative, as reported by tech-ish on 23 October, named no countries, no budget and no start date. Egypt's announcement, as reported by iAfrica, disclosed no capacity, cost or site for the planned data centres. South Africa's policy is not public: its drafting team must submit to the minister by 30 November, with internal consultation from December to February and a Cabinet submission in March 2027. Against that, the one multi-country GPU commitment this publication has been able to document with a primary source, the Cassava–Nvidia programme reported by Bloomberg in April 2025, remains at 3,000 GPUs delivered out of 12,000 planned.

This is a hypothesis built from announcements, not from outcomes. It should be read as a prompt to check, in the next two editions, whether any of these plans acquires a number, a location and a delivery date.

What would make this wrong
This thesis is wrong if, by the March 2027 issue, the World Bank initiative names participating countries, a budget and a start date, or Egypt discloses the site, capacity and financing of at least one of its planned data centres. Either would show that the October announcements were the front end of funded programmes rather than stand-alone statements.
Continuity: verdict on last month's thesis
Partly confirmed. September held that partnership instruments were moving faster than statutes. The statute half was strengthened this month: South Africa's drafting team set out a sequence (30 November submission to the minister, December to February internal consultation, March 2027 Cabinet submission to approve gazetting for public comment, then a second Cabinet approval with no date), which is slower than the May and July targets. The partnership half was not tested: this edition's searches found no programme-level agreement under the Kenya–Anthropic declaration, but the window is short and the March 2027 test set in September stands. No verdict of "abandoned" is warranted.

South Africa's policy team sets its timetable: 30 November draft, March 2027 Cabinet, public comment only afterwards

What happenedJeanette Morwane, acting deputy director-general at the Department of Communications and Digital Technologies and project manager for the policy, told a South Africa–Finland AI dialogue in Pretoria that the drafting team must submit a revised policy and a discussion document to the minister by 30 November. Internal government consultation runs December to February. Cabinet is due to receive the policy by March 2027, mainly to approve gazetting it for public comment; a second Cabinet approval follows, with no date given.
Why it mattersIt is the first dated sequence for the rebuild, and it shows that March 2027 is the start of public comment, not the end of the process. The target has moved from November 2026 (told to Parliament in May) to the end of the financial year (Minister Malatsi, September).
WatchWhether the 30 November submission is met, and whether the discussion document is published.

§4Deep Research Essay

The Plans Outnumber the Contracts: Africa's Compute Is Being Governed Before It Is Built

In one October, a multilateral lender, two governments and a policy team all described compute as shared or sovereign infrastructure. None gave a budget, a site and a date.

The World Bank's 20 October announcement in Seoul of an AI Infrastructure and Readiness Initiative is the clearest of the month's statements. It proposes shared GPU compute that countries can use on a pay-as-you-go basis, for Africa and Latin America, with Cassava Technologies and Nvidia as knowledge partners. The motivating figures are stark: sub-Saharan Africa holds roughly 18% of the world's population but, in the coverage reviewed, about 0.6% of global data-centre capacity. But the reporting is equally clear about what is missing. No participating countries were named, no budget was given, and no start date was set.

Egypt's statement of the same month has the same shape. The communications and IT minister told Egypt Forum 2026 that the state plans five to seven mega data centres over four to five years and intends to export compute by 2030. Targets accompany the plan: ICT at 8% of GDP and $12 billion in digital exports by 2030. As iAfrica reported it, no capacity, cost or site accompanied the plan. South Africa's version is a policy discussion. Jeanette Morwane, who manages the national AI policy project, said the drafting focus is compute and data sovereignty and also "model sovereignty", so that large language models cover South Africa's official languages, and that there is "a huge need for investing in computing infrastructure". Nigeria, for its part, proposed pooling African compute for large-scale AI work.

Set these beside what exists. The one multi-country GPU programme with a primary source is Cassava's, reported by Bloomberg in April 2025 at up to $720 million: 12,000 GPUs planned over three to four years across five countries, with 3,000 delivered first in South Africa. The Microsoft–G42 data centre in Kenya, which August's edition listed among Kenya's compute options, was reported by tech-ish in May as paused after President Ruto said running it would cut power to half the country. A plan that names a site, a financier and a power source is a project. A plan that names a goal is a position, and October was largely made of positions.

That is not a criticism of the positions. Public-sector framing often precedes finance: a lender announces an initiative, then countries apply, then budgets are set. The point of this essay is narrower. Governance language is moving ahead of capacity, and the sovereignty and sharing vocabulary now attached to compute will be applied to capacity that is, for the most part, still to be built. South Africa illustrates the sequencing risk: its policy will say how compute should be governed, but the draft will not be public until Cabinet approves it for comment in or after March 2027, and the Department's own slide, as TechCentral reported, concedes that regional peers are moving faster on implementation.

Evidence
World Bank initiative: tech-ish, 23 Oct 2026. Egypt: iAfrica, Oct 2026. South Africa: TechCentral, 29 Oct 2026. Cassava–Nvidia programme: Bloomberg, 7 Apr 2025. Kenya Microsoft–G42 pause: tech-ish, May 2026. Nigeria pooling proposal: iAfrica, Oct 2026 (headline-level; details not reviewed for this edition).
Strongest counterargument
Announcements are how public financing starts, and the World Bank initiative is days old; judging it for missing budgets is premature. Egypt has already licensed a $400 million data-centre project, so its state plan is not empty. The essay's claim is a hypothesis about the ratio of plans to contracts, to be rechecked as details emerge, not a finding that the plans are insubstantial.
Strategic implications
For investors: treat sovereignty and sharing announcements as signals of future procurement, and ask for site, power and financing before modelling demand. For policymakers: attach delivery milestones to compute statements, so that a plan can be checked. For operators: the World Bank's named partners, Cassava and Nvidia, are the only identified supply side so far.
What would change this view
A World Bank announcement naming countries and a budget, or an Egyptian data-centre project with a disclosed site and financing, before the March 2027 issue.

§6Commercial Strategy

The commercial signal this month is about measurement. TechCabal's Q3 figure ($1.025B) is 31% above its Q3 2025 figure ($785M) on 12% more deals; Disrupt Africa's count of $583M across 58 startups is a smaller universe by construction. Operators and investors using either number should cite the tracker. On the supply side of compute, the only named providers in this month's public-sector framing are Cassava and Nvidia (as World Bank knowledge partners), which gives a first indication of who a shared-compute programme might buy from. This edition makes no estimate of demand.
