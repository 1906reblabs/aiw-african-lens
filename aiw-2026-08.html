§1Editor's Thesis

Africa's AI infrastructure buildout has stopped being aspirational. Nvidia's money, Microsoft's money, Gulf-adjacent capital in Morocco and Egypt, and Chinese vendor financing in Zambia are now in the ground or under signed contract across at least six countries. Africa's AI governance buildout has not kept the same pace: South Africa's national policy has sat withdrawn since April, Nigeria's flagship digital-economy bill has missed two self-imposed deadlines, and INTERPOL now says AI-enabled crime is outrunning the law-enforcement capacity built to police it. The gap between the two curves — capital deployed versus rules written — is this month's story, and it is a real one, not a modelled scenario.

The infrastructure evidence is concrete and dated: Nvidia and Cassava Technologies' $700M GPU rollout, first announced March 2025, delivered its initial 3,000-GPU tranche to South Africa in June 2025 and is now mid-build on the remaining 12,000 GPUs across Egypt, Nigeria, Kenya and Morocco. Egypt's National Telecommunications Regulatory Authority licensed a $400M data-centre project to Hassan Allam Holding in June 2026, days after Egypt's electricity, ICT and investment ministries met to coordinate a national data-centre strategy. Morocco broke ground in April 2026 on a 12-billion-dirham (roughly $1.2B) AI Factory outside Casablanca targeting 36 megawatts of capacity by the end of 2027. None of this required a finished AI law in any of the three countries.

The governance evidence is just as concrete, and just as dated: South Africa's Draft National AI Policy was gazetted for public comment on 8 April 2026 and withdrawn on 26 April after News24 found fabricated academic citations in its reference list — a real scandal, independently confirmed by Reuters/CNBC Africa and South African legal commentary from Baker McKenzie and Fasken. A seven-member independent panel chaired by Professor Benjamin Rosman of the University of the Witwatersrand is now rebuilding the document, with Cabinet resubmission targeted for November 2026 and public comment reopening only in January 2027 — nine months after the original withdrawal. Nigeria's National Digital Economy and E-Governance Bill, expected by Bloomberg and Tech Hive Advisory to pass by the end of Q1 and then Q2 2026 respectively, remained a proposed framework as of the most recent legal commentary available, with no confirmed passage date. Meanwhile INTERPOL's African Cyberthreat Assessment Report 2026, released 3 August 2026, found AI now enables 55% of reported African cybercrime and that 92% of surveyed law-enforcement agencies lack AI-specific readiness.

What Would Make This Wrong

This thesis is wrong if South Africa's independent panel delivers a revised policy to Cabinet on or ahead of its stated November 2026 target and the January 2027 comment window opens as planned — in which case the "governance is lagging" framing would need to be revised to "governance is slower but not stalled." It is also wrong if Nigeria's Digital Economy and E-Governance Bill passes before this publication's next edition, closing the gap between announced deadlines and actual enactment that has defined its 2026 trajectory so far.

§4Deep Research Essay
GeopoliticsInfrastructureCapital

The Continent Building the Chips Is Not the Continent Selling the Cobalt — Yet

Every African AI infrastructure announcement this year sits downstream of a mineral export policy written in Kinshasa. That fact has been true for years. What changed in 2026 is that the DRC started acting on it.

Read Egypt's $400M data-centre licence, Morocco's $1.2B AI Factory, and the Cassava-Nvidia five-country GPU rollout as three separate national success stories and you miss the thing they share: every GPU, every server rack, every piece of AI-ready compute landing in Cairo, Casablanca, Lagos, Nairobi or Johannesburg this year depends on a semiconductor supply chain that runs, at its base, through cobalt, coltan and copper extracted overwhelmingly from one country that is not on that list of infrastructure winners. The Democratic Republic of Congo supplies roughly 70 to 80 percent of the world's mined cobalt, by the range of estimates in current reporting from Mining.com and the IEA's Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026, and holds vast coltan and copper reserves alongside it. For most of the past decade, that endowment functioned as a commodity fact, priced and shipped like any other raw material. In 2026, the DRC government began treating it as a policy lever instead.

The sequence is documented and dated. A temporary cobalt export suspension in 2025 gave way to a quota regime managed by ARECOMS, the DRC's mining regulatory authority. Cobalt prices, which had fallen from nearly $95,000 a tonne in 2018 to around $21,000 in early 2025 under oversupply conditions, climbed back to just over $56,000 a tonne as the quotas bit. In April, per reporting picked up by Mining.com, Kinshasa announced plans to build state-controlled stockpiles of cobalt, coltan and germanium — funded through buybacks of mining companies' existing stock — explicitly framed around applications in semiconductors, fibre optics, infrared systems and defence technology, not just batteries and consumer electronics. Most recently, restrictions on copper and cobalt exports have tightened further, aimed at pushing more processing onto Congolese soil rather than shipping raw ore. None of this is speculative; it is a documented trajectory from a 2025 suspension, through 2025–26 quotas, to a 2026 stockpile-and-restriction policy explicitly aimed at capturing more value before the ore leaves the country.

The great powers noticed before most AI infrastructure coverage did. In February 2026 the United States hosted its first Critical Minerals Ministerial, and the US-backed Orion Critical Mineral Consortium signed a deal, reported at roughly $9 billion, to acquire a 40 percent stake in two of Glencore's largest Congolese copper and cobalt operations — Mutanda Mining and Kamoto Copper Company — with the consortium gaining rights to direct sales to nominated buyers while Glencore retained operational control. That agreement followed a June 2025 peace deal between the DRC and Rwanda that, per Foreign Policy's analysis, secured preferential US access to Congolese tantalum, gold, cobalt, copper and lithium as part of its terms. China, whose firms have spent roughly a decade building cobalt stockpiles of their own, is the implicit reference case Kinshasa's own officials cite for the value of strategic reserves. This is not a two-country story; it is now a live multi-power negotiation over the physical inputs that every AI infrastructure headline in this issue quietly assumes will keep arriving on schedule.

What this means for the African AI infrastructure buildout described elsewhere in this edition is a dependency most coverage of it does not name. Egypt's data-centre strategy, Morocco's AI Factory, and the Cassava-Nvidia rollout are all downstream consumers of a global chip supply chain whose upstream cost and availability is now more directly a function of Congolese policy than at any point in the past decade. None of the five countries in this issue's infrastructure buildout is itself a major cobalt, coltan or copper producer at DRC's scale — the leverage sits in Kinshasa, not in the countries doing the building. If the DRC's export discipline holds and cobalt prices continue climbing, or if further restrictions follow the copper and cobalt tightening already reported, the cost base underneath every AI data-centre build-out on this continent moves with it, whether or not the country doing the building has any policy lever of its own to respond with.

Evidence

DRC cobalt share of global production (approx. 70–80%), IEA Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 and Mining.com reporting (26–27 June 2026); cobalt price trajectory ($95,000/t in 2018 to ~$21,000/t in early 2025 to over $56,000/t by mid-2026), Geomechanics.io (26 June 2026); ARECOMS quota regime and 2026 stockpile plans for cobalt, coltan and germanium, Mining.com; February 2026 US Critical Minerals Ministerial and the ~$9B Orion Consortium stake in Mutanda Mining and Kamoto Copper Company, OpenDemocracy (21 April 2026); June 2025 DRC–Rwanda peace deal terms on US mineral access, referenced in ongoing 2026 coverage; recent copper/cobalt export tightening, reporting reviewed 22 August 2026.

The Strongest Counterargument

The counterargument is that this dependency is old news dressed up as a new insight — analysts have flagged Congolese mineral concentration risk for AI hardware for years, and the infrastructure buildouts covered in this issue are proceeding regardless, which suggests the market has already priced the risk or judges it manageable. It's also true that cobalt's role varies by chip type and that battery and defence-sector demand, not AI-specific chip demand, is the primary driver of the price moves cited here — this essay should not be read as claiming AI demand specifically is moving cobalt prices.

Strategic Implications

For infrastructure investors: model DRC export-policy risk as a genuine input-cost variable for African AI infrastructure timelines, not a background geopolitical curiosity — the price trajectory above is already a five-year high. For policymakers in Egypt, Morocco, Kenya, Nigeria and South Africa: none of the national AI strategies reviewed for this edition name upstream mineral-supply exposure as a risk factor, which is a genuine gap given how directly their compute ambitions now depend on it. For readers of this publication specifically: the DRC will recur as a subject in future editions not because it hosts AI infrastructure — it does not, in the sources reviewed — but because it is the physical chokepoint underneath everyone else's.

§5Engineering Frontier

⚡ Power

Egypt's national data-centre coordination effort explicitly pairs compute planning with power planning: the June 2026 ministerial meeting at the New Administrative Capital brought Electricity and Renewable Energy together with ICT and Investment specifically to map power-ready sites before, not after, investment commitments. Nigeria's push is running the opposite way: reporting on Nigeria's first AI-focused data centres notes AI facilities can draw up to 20 times the power of conventional server halls, with operators exploring gas and geothermal alternatives to a national grid already under strain.

🧱 Chips

The Cassava-Nvidia rollout (3,000 GPUs delivered to South Africa in June 2025; 12,000 more planned across Egypt, Nigeria, Kenya and Morocco) is the largest single confirmed GPU commitment in the sources reviewed. It sits, per this issue's Deep Research Essay, downstream of a DRC cobalt and coltan export regime that has hardened through 2026. No source reviewed for this edition disclosed the specific chip generation involved in the remaining four-country rollout.

🌐 Infrastructure

Kenya's compute layer is now three-way contested: a roughly 100MW G42-linked facility, a Microsoft-G42 package reported at $1B including a green Azure East Africa Cloud Region and Swahili/English LLM development, and an Oracle Cloud Infrastructure region in Nairobi confirmed by iXAfrica at GITEX Kenya in May 2026. Nigeria opened what regional coverage describes as West Africa's first hyperscale-ready AI data centre in Lagos during Q2 2026, alongside a Nigeria AI Scaling Hub for shared national compute.

Dependency Watch
Zambia's national AI data centre, developed with Huawei and Smart Zambia, is the one major 2026 African AI infrastructure commitment in this issue's source base that follows the state-led Chinese financing model rather than the Nvidia/Cassava private-sector model or the Gulf-adjacent sovereign-capital model seen in Morocco and Egypt — worth tracking as a third distinct financing architecture on the continent, alongside the private-capital and sovereign-capital tracks.
§6Commercial Strategy

The clearest commercial-strategy signal this window is compositional, not aggregate: of the $818M in H1 2026 African startup equity tracked by TechCabal Insights, debt financing ($614M) is now nearly as large a share of total capital as equity — a shift TechCabal's own reporting attributes to founders in asset-heavy sectors (electric mobility, solar, lending books) choosing loans over dilution. AI-specific deal flow within that total remains modest in absolute terms: Nairobi's AethexAI raised a $3M pre-seed for customer-support and voice-automation tooling, and Morocco's Davis AI raised $5.5M for AI-driven real-estate tools. Neither approaches the scale of the infrastructure commitments covered in Section 5, underscoring that 2026's African AI capital story is an infrastructure story first and an applications-funding story a distant second.

Capital Flow Read

Egypt and South Africa led disclosed Q1 2026 country funding ($154M and $134M respectively, per TechCabal Insights), with Kenya and Nigeria close behind — a spread that roughly tracks the same four countries carrying the bulk of this issue's infrastructure commitments, suggesting capital allocators are treating policy uncertainty (acute in South Africa, chronic in Nigeria) as a secondary factor behind market size and existing compute access.

For the Operator — Build / Buy / Partner

Build: African-language and sector-specific AI applications remain comparatively under-capitalised relative to infrastructure — AethexAI's and Davis AI's rounds are the only AI-labelled deals of meaningful size surfaced in this edition's research, a gap worth testing rather than assuming is structural. Buy: access to Cassava's South African GPU cluster is already available via its MoU with the South African AI Association; equivalent access in the four pending rollout countries is not yet confirmed as live. Partner: Egypt's and Kenya's parallel infrastructure-and-policy sequencing (data-centre licensing alongside active AI-policy drafting) make both markets more legible for near-term partnership than South Africa, where the underlying national policy will not exist in finalised form before 2027 at the earliest.
